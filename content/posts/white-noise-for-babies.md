White noise is generally considered safe for babies when it is kept at a low volume, placed well away from the crib, and not blasted for hours on end. The main risk is loudness: some sound machines can get loud enough at full volume and close range to exceed recommended noise limits for infants. Used gently, white noise can help many babies settle and stay asleep through household sounds.

## Why white noise helps many babies sleep

Babies often settle well to steady sound. A few reasons are commonly given:

- **It masks sudden noises.** A door closing, a dog barking, or a sibling laughing is less likely to startle a baby when there is a steady background sound.
- **It becomes a sleep cue.** Over time, the sound can signal "it is time to sleep," much like a bath or a bedtime song.
- **It may feel familiar.** The womb is not a silent place, and many parents find steady whooshing sounds calming for newborns.

Not every baby responds to it, and that is fine. Some sleep perfectly well in a quiet room.

## The safety concern: volume

The main concern with white noise for babies is volume, not the sound itself.

A 2014 study in the journal Pediatrics tested a range of infant sleep machines and found that some of them, when played at maximum volume and placed close to the crib, produced sound levels above the limits recommended for infants in hospital nurseries. The researchers raised concerns that long exposure at those levels could potentially affect a baby's developing hearing, and they suggested parents place machines as far from the crib as practical, keep the volume low, and limit how long the machine runs.

That does not mean white noise is dangerous. It means the settings matter. Most of the risk comes from turning a machine all the way up and putting it right next to a baby's head.

> A good rule of thumb: if you have to raise your voice to talk over the sound in the nursery, it is too loud.

## Safe use: volume, distance and placement

The American Academy of Pediatrics has echoed the study's advice to keep sound machines away from the crib and at a low volume. In practice, that looks like this:

1. **Keep it well away from the crib.** Place the machine or speaker across the room, not on the crib rail or inside the crib. Never put a phone, speaker, or device inside the sleep space.
2. **Start at the lowest volume.** Turn it up only until household sounds are softened, then stop. Quieter is better.
3. **Check the level where your baby sleeps.** Stand by the crib and listen. A free sound meter app on your phone can give you a rough reading, although phone apps are not perfectly accurate.
4. **Avoid maximum volume.** Even if the machine goes louder, you rarely need more than a fraction of its range.
5. **Keep cords and devices out of reach.** Anything with a cable should be well away from the crib and secured.
6. **Follow safe sleep basics.** White noise does not replace the essentials: baby on their back, on a firm flat surface, in a crib free of pillows, bumpers, and loose blankets.

For more detail on judging loudness, see [how loud white noise should be](/blog/how-loud-should-white-noise-be/).

## All night or just to fall asleep?

Parents often wonder whether to leave white noise on all night. There is no single right answer.

Running it continuously can help babies stay asleep through early morning noises and resettle between sleep cycles. On the other hand, the 2014 Pediatrics study suggested limiting the length of exposure, and some parents simply prefer a quieter room once their baby is asleep.

A reasonable middle ground is to use a sleep timer: let the sound run while your baby falls asleep and through the first stretch of the night, then fade out. If your baby tends to wake when it stops, you can try a longer timer or keep it running at a very low level. Our guide on [sleep sounds all night or on a timer](/blog/sleep-sounds-all-night-or-timer/) goes through the pros and cons. If you are unsure what is right for your child, ask your pediatrician.

## White, pink or brown noise for babies?

Most baby machines play white noise, but there is nothing special about it. Pink and brown noise are softer and less hissy, and some parents find them more pleasant to hear through the baby monitor. Gentle rain or ocean sounds work for many babies too.

What matters is that the sound is steady and quiet. Avoid tracks with sudden changes, loud thunder, birdsong, or music that rises and falls, since those can be more stimulating than soothing. See [white vs pink vs brown noise](/blog/white-pink-brown-noise/) to hear the differences.

## How to wean your baby off white noise

Some parents worry their child will become dependent on white noise. In reality, a sleep cue is not a problem unless it becomes inconvenient for your family, for example when traveling or staying with relatives.

If you decide to stop, a gradual approach usually goes smoothly:

1. **Lower the volume a little every few nights** over two to four weeks.
2. **Shorten the timer** so the sound stops sooner after your child falls asleep.
3. **Swap in another cue** such as a consistent bedtime story, song, or night light routine.
4. **Pick a calm stretch** rather than a week with travel, teething, illness, or a big change.

There is no set age to stop. Many families wean during the toddler years; others keep a quiet fan or sound running for years with no issues.

## When to talk to a doctor

Talk to your pediatrician if your baby has trouble sleeping most nights, seems unusually unsettled, snores or pauses in breathing during sleep, or if you have any concerns about their hearing. A sound machine is a comfort tool, not a fix for underlying sleep or health problems.

## The short version

White noise can be a helpful, gentle sleep aid for babies when you keep it quiet, keep it across the room, and avoid running it at full volume for hours. Use a timer if you prefer, choose a steady sound without sudden changes, and wean gradually whenever it stops suiting your family.
