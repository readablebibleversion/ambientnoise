The most reliable way to block out snoring is to combine two things: earplugs to cut the loudest peaks, and a steady low-frequency sound like brown noise to mask what still gets through. Snoring is hard to ignore because it starts and stops, so a constant sound that "fills in" the silence between snores makes each one far less jarring. If the snoring is very loud or comes with gasping or pauses in breathing, the snorer should also talk to a doctor.

## Why snoring is so hard to sleep through

Plenty of noises are louder than a snore. What makes snoring uniquely annoying is its rhythm. Your brain is very good at noticing change, and a snore is a cycle of quiet, then a rising rasp, then quiet again. Each new snore is a small "event" that can nudge you toward waking, especially in lighter stages of sleep.

That is also why sound masking works. You are not trying to drown the snore out with something louder. You are trying to raise the background level so the gap between silence and snore is smaller. When the room is never fully quiet, the start of each snore stands out less, and your brain has less reason to pay attention.

## Brown noise vs pink noise for snoring

Snoring lives mostly in the low and low-middle frequencies: a deep, vibrating rumble with some rattling texture on top. To mask a sound well, you want a masking sound with energy in the same range.

- **Brown noise** puts most of its energy in the low end. It sounds like a deep roar or a distant waterfall, and it is usually the best match for a heavy, rumbling snorer.
- **Pink noise** is more balanced. It covers the lows but also has more middle and upper content, so it can help with the raspy, higher-pitched part of some snores.
- **White noise** has a lot of high-frequency hiss. It can work, but many people find it harsh at the volume needed to cover snoring.

If you are not sure which to use, start with brown noise and add a little pink if the snore still "cuts through." ## Layer a natural sound on top

A pure noise color is great at masking, but some people find it monotonous. Layering a gentle natural sound on top can make the mix easier to fall asleep to and adds texture that helps hide the "edges" of a snore.

Good companions for snoring include:

- **Steady rain**, which adds a soft, irregular patter in the middle frequencies.
- **Wind**, which brings slow swells that blend well with breathing sounds.
- **A stream or waterfall**, which is close to brown noise but feels more natural.

## How to pair noise with earplugs

Earplugs are the other half of the solution. They reduce how loud everything is, while noise fills in the quiet. Together they can be surprisingly effective.

1. **Choose comfortable earplugs.** Soft foam earplugs block a lot of sound but must be rolled and inserted properly to work. Silicone or wax earplugs sit over the ear canal and suit people who find foam uncomfortable.
2. **Insert them well.** With foam plugs, roll them thin, gently pull the top of your ear up and back, and hold the plug in place while it expands. A poorly fitted plug blocks much less.
3. **Play your masking sound from a speaker, not headphones.** A small speaker on the nightstand, ideally between you and the snorer, is easier to sleep with than earbuds. Some people use sleep headphones in a soft headband instead.
4. **Keep the volume modest.** With earplugs in, you need less masking volume than you might think. Our guide on how loud white noise should be covers sensible levels.

If earplugs bother your ears, cause pain, or you get frequent ear infections, check with a doctor or pharmacist before using them every night.

## Other practical tips for sleeping next to a snorer

Sound is only one part of the picture. A few small changes can reduce how much snoring reaches you in the first place.

- **Fall asleep first.** If you can get to sleep before the snorer, you are more likely to stay asleep. Going to bed a little earlier can help.
- **Change the snorer's position.** Many people snore more on their back. Sleeping on the side, or with the head slightly raised, helps some snorers.
- **Look at evening habits.** Alcohol close to bedtime and a blocked nose often make snoring worse. These are worth gentle discussion, not blame.
- **Add soft furnishings.** Curtains, rugs and upholstered headboards absorb some sound rather than bouncing it around a bare room.
- **Consider separate rooms on bad nights.** Sleeping apart sometimes is common and practical. It does not have to say anything about the relationship, and it can protect both people's sleep.

## When snoring is a medical issue

Occasional snoring is very common and usually harmless. But loud, regular snoring can sometimes be a sign of **obstructive sleep apnea**, a condition where the airway repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep.

Signs that are worth raising with a doctor include:

- Gasping, choking or snorting sounds during sleep
- Pauses in breathing that a partner notices
- Feeling very sleepy during the day, even after a full night in bed
- Morning headaches or waking with a dry mouth

Sleep apnea is treatable, and treatment often reduces snoring a lot, which helps both people. Sound masking is a great tool for the person trying to sleep, but it should not replace getting the snorer checked if any of these signs are present. Talk to a doctor if you are concerned.

## The short version

To block out snoring, combine decent earplugs with a steady low-frequency sound such as brown noise, keep the volume moderate, and add a gentle natural layer like rain if pure noise feels flat. Help the situation where you can with sleep position, earlier bedtimes and soft furnishings. And if the snoring comes with gasping, pauses in breathing or heavy daytime tiredness, encourage the snorer to see a doctor.
