White noise for sleep should be soft: about as loud as a gentle shower in the next room, and quiet enough that you can easily hold a normal conversation over it. For babies, keep it even lower and place the speaker well away from the crib. The goal is to blur disruptive noises, not to drown out the whole world.

## Why volume matters more than the sound

People spend a lot of time choosing between white, pink and brown noise, then crank the volume up without thinking. Volume is the part that actually affects comfort and safety.

Sound masking works by reducing the contrast between background and sudden noises. A dog bark or a slammed door wakes you because it jumps out of a quiet room. A steady layer of sound makes that jump smaller, and it does not need to be loud to do that. In most bedrooms, a soft level is enough to take the edge off traffic, a partner's movements or a noisy hallway.

Louder sound has downsides. It can be tiring to listen to, it may make the room feel less restful, and long exposure to loud sound is linked to hearing damage. Health agencies generally associate risk with sustained levels around 85 decibels and above, roughly the level of heavy city traffic. A sleep sound should be nowhere near that.

## A safe volume for adults

For most adults, a comfortable sleep sound sits somewhere between a whisper and a quiet conversation at the distance of your bed. You will rarely need more than that.

Signs your volume is about right:

- You can talk to someone in the room in a normal voice without straining
- The sound fades into the background after a few minutes
- Nighttime noises are softened, even if you can still faintly hear them
- You do not feel a sense of pressure or fatigue in your ears in the morning

If you are trying to cover something specific, such as snoring or loud neighbors, it is tempting to go louder. Before you do, try changing the *type* of sound instead. Deeper sounds like brown noise are better at covering low rumbles, while brighter sounds cover voices and higher-pitched noise. ## A safe volume for babies

Many parents use white noise for babies, and it can be a helpful part of a sleep routine. The key is to keep it quiet and keep it at a distance.

A 2014 study in the journal Pediatrics tested a selection of infant sleep machines and found that some could exceed recommended noise limits for infants at their maximum volume, especially when placed close to the crib. The study did not show that white noise is harmful at low levels, but it was a clear reminder that the volume dial matters.

Following American Academy of Pediatrics guidance, sensible habits include:

1. **Place the machine away from the crib.** Put it across the room, not on the crib rail or next to your baby's head. Distance lowers the level that reaches their ears.
2. **Use the lowest setting that works.** Start very low and only raise it a little if needed.
3. **Avoid maximum volume.** A machine that is quiet at a low setting can still be loud at its top setting.
4. **Consider a timer.** Some families switch the sound off once the baby is asleep, which limits total exposure.

For more on routines and safety, read our guide to [white noise for babies](/blog/white-noise-for-babies/). If you have concerns about your baby's hearing or sleep, talk to your pediatrician.

## How to judge loudness without a meter

You do not need special equipment to get the level roughly right. These simple checks work well.

- **The conversation test.** Stand in the middle of the room and talk to someone. If you can speak comfortably in your normal voice, the volume is reasonable. If you have to raise your voice, it is too loud.
- **The pillow test.** Lie down where you will sleep. The sound should feel like part of the room, not something aimed at you.
- **The doorway test.** Step outside the room and close the door. If you can clearly hear the sound from the hallway, it may be louder than you need.
- **The next-morning test.** If you wake up with ringing ears or a sense of fullness, turn it down.

Free sound meter apps on phones can give a rough reading if you are curious, but their accuracy varies between devices. Treat any number as a ballpark, and trust the conversation test.

## Speaker placement makes a big difference

Where you put the speaker changes how loud the sound actually is at your ears. Sound gets noticeably quieter as you move away from the source, so a few feet of distance can matter more than a few clicks on the volume dial.

- **Keep it off the pillow.** A phone right next to your head delivers far more sound to your ears than the same phone on a dresser.
- **Point it into the room.** Aiming the speaker at a wall or into open space spreads the sound more evenly.
- **Place it between you and the noise.** If the noise comes from a window or shared wall, putting the speaker on that side can help mask it at a lower volume.
- **Be cautious with earbuds.** Sleeping in earbuds puts sound directly into your ear canal. If you use them, keep the volume very low and consider a timer.

## Settling on your level

Your ideal volume might change night to night. A quiet weekend may need almost nothing, while a stormy night or a noisy street may need slightly more. That is fine, as long as you stay in the soft range. When in doubt, go quieter: most people find that a lower level than they expected works just as well once they get used to it.
