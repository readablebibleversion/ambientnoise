Brown noise is a steady, random sound with most of its energy in the low frequencies, so it sounds like a deep, soft rumble: think distant thunder, a strong river, or the hum inside an airplane cabin. It is a close cousin of white and pink noise, just tilted much further toward the bass. People use it to sleep, to focus, and to cover low sounds like traffic or snoring.

## What brown noise actually is

All "colored" noises are random sounds made of many frequencies at once. What changes is how the energy is spread out.

- **White noise** has equal energy at every frequency and sounds bright and hissy.
- **Pink noise** reduces the higher frequencies so every octave carries equal energy. It sounds like steady rain.
- **Brown noise** reduces the high end even more steeply, so the low frequencies dominate.

The name has nothing to do with the color brown. It comes from Brownian motion, the random jittering of tiny particles first described by the botanist Robert Brown. The math behind that random motion produces a signal with exactly this deep, low-heavy profile. You will sometimes see it called red noise, which means the same thing in everyday use.

If you want a side-by-side comparison of all three, our guide to [white vs pink vs brown noise](/blog/white-pink-brown-noise/) goes through each one.

## What brown noise sounds like

On a good pair of headphones, brown noise sounds smooth and enveloping. There is very little hiss. Common comparisons include:

- The cabin of a plane at cruising altitude
- A waterfall or river heard from a little way off
- Heavy surf rolling in the distance
- A strong wind outside a closed window

Because it lacks the sharp top end of white noise, many people describe it as "warm" or "cozy." On small phone or laptop speakers it can sound thinner, since those speakers struggle to reproduce deep bass. Headphones or a decent speaker make a real difference.

## Why brown noise went viral

Brown noise had a moment on social media in 2022, when short videos of people listening to it racked up huge view counts. A lot of the excitement came from people with ADHD describing how it seemed to quiet their racing thoughts and help them get started on tasks. Others simply found it more pleasant than white noise for sleep.

Part of the appeal is easy to understand. Brown noise is soothing in a way that is hard to describe until you hear it, and it is free and easy to try. The online buzz, though, ran well ahead of the research.

## What the research says (and does not say)

This is where it is worth being careful. There is very little scientific research looking at brown noise specifically.

Most of the studies on noise and attention have used white noise. Some of that research has suggested that a moderate level of background noise might help certain people with attention difficulties perform better on some tasks, while other studies have found no benefit or found it depends heavily on the person. Overall, the evidence is limited and mixed.

So the honest summary is:

- Lots of people report that brown noise helps them focus or relax.
- That experience is real for them, and it costs nothing to try.
- It has not been shown to treat ADHD, anxiety, insomnia, or any other condition.

> Brown noise can be a helpful tool, but it is not a treatment. If you are managing ADHD, sleep problems, or anxiety, talk to a doctor about your options.

## Who tends to like brown noise

From what people commonly report, brown noise suits:

- **People who find white noise harsh.** If white noise feels like static that grates after an hour, brown is often the fix.
- **Light sleepers bothered by low sounds.** Traffic rumble, a neighbor's music through the wall, or a partner's snoring are all low-frequency sounds that brown noise can help blend away.
- **People who want a "wall" of sound while working.** Its steady, featureless quality makes it easy to tune out.
- **Frequent flyers.** If you tend to doze off on planes, brown noise recreates that cabin feeling at home.

It is less effective at covering high, sharp sounds like a beeping phone or a dog's bark. For those, a bit of pink or white noise layered on top helps.

## How to use brown noise well

A few practical tips get the most out of it:

1. **Start quieter than you think.** Low sounds can feel deceptively gentle, which makes it easy to turn them up too far. Pick the lowest level that covers what you are trying to block.
2. **Use headphones for focus, a speaker for sleep.** Headphones bring out the bass and block more of the room. For sleep, a speaker across the room is more comfortable and lets you hear alarms.
3. **Layer it with nature.** Brown noise under light rain or a stream gives you depth plus a little texture. Try the [rain over brown noise mix](/#mix=brown:0.5,rain:0.4).
4. **Set a timer if you only need help falling asleep.** Some people sleep better with sound that fades out after half an hour or so.
5. **Give it a few days.** A new sleep sound can feel odd at first. Your brain usually learns to ignore it quickly.

## Is brown noise safe?

For most adults, listening at a comfortable volume is generally considered fine. The main risk with any sound is volume. If you have to raise your voice to talk over it, it is too loud, and long hours of loud sound through headphones can contribute to hearing damage over time. Our guide on [how loud white noise should be](/blog/how-loud-should-white-noise-be/) covers this in more detail and applies equally to brown noise.

If you are using it for a baby, keep it low and the speaker well away from the crib, and check with your pediatrician if you have concerns.

## The short version

Brown noise is a deep, rumbly version of white noise, with the hiss stripped out and the bass turned up. It went viral thanks to people who said it helped them focus and sleep, and many people genuinely enjoy it, but the research on it is thin and it is not a treatment for anything. Try it at a low volume and see whether it works for you.
