Background noise may help some people with ADHD focus, though the research is limited and mixed. Small studies suggest steady noise, such as white noise, can improve performance on some tasks for some people with attention difficulties, while having no effect or even a negative effect for others. Brown noise is popular online, but there is very little research on it specifically, so treat it as a tool to experiment with, not a treatment.

## Why noise might help a busy brain

It sounds backwards: if you struggle with focus, why add more sound? Many people with ADHD describe silence as anything but calm. In a quiet room, every small noise, stray thought or itch can pull attention away. A steady layer of sound can make the environment feel more even and, for some, easier to settle into.

Researchers have offered an explanation for this. One idea, sometimes called the moderate brain arousal model, suggests that people with ADHD may sometimes be under-stimulated, and that a certain amount of steady background noise could bring them closer to a level where focus comes more easily. Too little stimulation and attention wanders; too much and it becomes overwhelming. The theory is still debated, but it fits what many people report.

## What the research actually says

The research here is real but limited, so it is worth being careful.

A 2007 study in the Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry found that white noise improved memory task performance in a small group of schoolchildren who were rated as inattentive, while children rated as attentive performed slightly worse with the noise. That contrast became an important idea in the field: background noise may help some people and hinder others.

Since then, several small studies have looked at white and pink noise in children and adults with ADHD symptoms. Reviews that have pooled these studies have generally found a small possible benefit for people with ADHD or attention difficulties on certain lab tasks, alongside a lot of uncertainty. Common limitations include:

- Small numbers of participants
- Short, lab-based tasks rather than real schoolwork or jobs
- Different noise types and volumes from study to study
- Limited research in adults

In short, there is enough evidence to make noise worth trying, but not enough to say it will work for you or to recommend it as a treatment.

## The brown noise trend

In recent years, brown noise has become something of a phenomenon on social media, with many people with ADHD describing it as a way to quiet a racing mind. Brown noise is deeper and softer than white noise, with more energy in the low frequencies, so it sounds like a low rumble, distant waterfall or strong wind. Our explainer on [what brown noise is](/blog/what-is-brown-noise/) goes deeper.

Why might people prefer it? Many find white noise harsh or hissy over long periods, while brown noise feels warmer and easier to tolerate for hours. That comfort alone can make it more usable.

What we do not know is whether brown noise works better than white or pink noise for attention. Most of the research has used white or pink noise, and personal stories, however common, are not the same as controlled studies. If brown noise feels good and helps you get things done, that is a perfectly good reason to use it. Just be aware that the enthusiasm online is ahead of the science.

## Practical tips for trying noise

If you want to see whether noise helps you, a little structure goes a long way.

1. **Start with a specific task.** Pick a type of work you find hard to start or stick with, such as reading, writing or admin.
2. **Try different sounds.** Test brown, pink and white noise on different days. Some people prefer nature sounds like rain or a stream. See our guide to [white, pink and brown noise](/blog/white-pink-brown-noise/) for the differences.
3. **Keep the volume moderate.** You want a steady background, not something that drowns out your thoughts. If you use headphones for long periods, keep the volume comfortable.
4. **Use time blocks.** Pair noise with short work sessions, such as 20 to 30 minutes, followed by a break. Starting the sound can become a cue that it is time to focus.
5. **Avoid sounds with events.** Thunder, birds and voices have moments that can grab attention. For focus, steady sounds usually work better.
6. **Keep a quick log.** Note what you played and how the session went. After a week or two, patterns tend to show up.
7. **Stop if it is not helping.** If noise makes you more scattered, tense or tired, silence or different sounds may suit you better.

## Where noise fits, and where it does not

Background noise is, at best, one small part of a focus toolkit. It can sit alongside other environmental strategies many people with ADHD find useful, such as clearing visual clutter, putting your phone in another room, breaking big tasks into small steps and working with a body double.

It is not a treatment for ADHD and should not replace professional care. Evidence-based treatments include medication, behavioral strategies, coaching and therapy, depending on age and needs. If you think you or your child might have ADHD, or your current approach is not working, talk to a doctor or a qualified specialist. They can help you find a plan that fits.

Parents should also take care with volume and headphones for children. Keep sound at a moderate level, and use speakers in the room where possible.

## The short version

Some people with ADHD find steady background noise makes it easier to focus, and small studies suggest it may help certain people on certain tasks. Brown noise is popular and comfortable, but there is little research on it specifically.
