Pink noise is a steady, random sound that contains all audible frequencies, but with the higher ones turned down so that each octave carries the same energy. To most ears it sounds like steady rain, wind in the trees, or a gentle waterfall: fuller and softer than the bright hiss of white noise. People use it for sleep, relaxation, focus, and masking background noise.

## What pink noise is, in plain terms

All the "noise colors" are random sounds spread across many frequencies. The difference is the balance.

White noise has equal energy at every individual frequency. Because there are far more high frequencies than low ones in each octave, white noise ends up sounding top-heavy and hissy.

Pink noise corrects for that. It gently reduces power as pitch rises, so the octave from 100 to 200 Hz has the same energy as the octave from 1,000 to 2,000 Hz. Since human hearing works roughly in octaves, pink noise sounds even and natural rather than sharp.

Brown noise goes further still, tilting heavily toward the bass for a deep rumble. Pink sits between the two. For a full side-by-side, see [white vs pink vs brown noise](/blog/white-pink-brown-noise/).

## What pink noise sounds like

If you play white noise and then switch to pink, the change is immediate. The hiss softens, and the sound seems to move lower and fill the room more evenly. Common comparisons include:

- Steady rain on a roof or window
- Wind moving through leaves
- A waterfall or fountain heard from nearby
- The rush of a shower

Many natural sounds have a roughly pink character, which may explain why they feel so familiar and easy to tune out.

## Everyday uses for pink noise

People reach for pink noise in a few common situations:

- **Falling asleep.** It covers household sounds and street noise without the edge of white noise.
- **Masking voices.** In open offices or shared homes, pink noise can make nearby conversations less intelligible and less distracting.
- **Focus.** It is a popular middle ground for people who find white noise tiring and brown noise too heavy.
- **Calming a busy mind.** A steady, featureless sound gives your attention something neutral to rest on.
- **Audio testing.** Sound engineers use pink noise to tune speakers and rooms because of its even balance across octaves.

## The deep sleep research, carefully explained

Pink noise gets a lot of attention because of research on deep sleep. It is worth understanding what that research actually did, because it is often overstated.

During deep sleep, the brain produces slow waves, large rhythmic patterns of activity linked to restful sleep and to memory consolidation. Some researchers have tried using sound to strengthen those waves.

A 2013 study in the journal Neuron found that short bursts of pink noise, timed precisely to the rhythm of a sleeper's slow brain waves, enhanced that slow-wave activity and improved memory recall the next morning in a small group of adults. A later study at Northwestern University reported a similar approach in a small group of older adults. Other research groups have explored the idea too, with mixed results.

Here are the important caveats:

1. **The sound was timed to brain activity.** These studies used equipment that monitored brain waves in real time and played brief clicks of pink noise at exactly the right moment. A speaker playing pink noise all night is not doing that.
2. **The studies were small.** They involved a modest number of people in sleep labs, not large populations at home.
3. **The effects were specific.** Researchers measured changes in brain waves and memory tests, not "better sleep" in general.

> Continuous pink noise may help you fall asleep by masking disruptive sounds, but it has not been shown to boost deep sleep the way timed lab stimulation can.

If you want to go further into the science, we cover it in more detail in [pink noise and deep sleep research](/blog/pink-noise-deep-sleep-research/). And if you have ongoing trouble sleeping, talk to a doctor; persistent insomnia and sleep apnea are worth getting checked.

## How to use pink noise for sleep

The most realistic benefit of pink noise at home is simple: it gives your brain a steady background so sudden sounds are less likely to jolt you awake. To make the most of it:

1. **Keep the volume low.** It should blend into the room, not dominate it. If you need to raise your voice to talk over it, turn it down.
2. **Place the speaker away from your head.** Across the room or on a dresser works better than right beside your pillow.
3. **Decide between all night and a timer.** Some people sleep best with continuous sound, others only need it to drift off. Our guide on [sleep sounds all night or on a timer](/blog/sleep-sounds-all-night-or-timer/) walks through the trade-offs.
4. **Layer in nature if pure noise feels flat.** Pink noise under soft rain adds a little texture without adding distraction. Try the [pink noise with soft rain mix](/#mix=pink:0.5,rain:0.3).
5. **Give it a week.** New sleep sounds can take a few nights to stop feeling noticeable.

## Is pink noise safe?

At a comfortable, low volume, pink noise is generally considered fine for adults. As with any sound, the main thing to watch is loudness and how long you listen through headphones. For babies, keep any sound machine low and well away from the crib, and ask your pediatrician if you have concerns.

## The short version

Pink noise is a balanced, softer version of white noise that sounds like steady rain or wind. It is a great all-round choice for sleep, focus, and masking noise. Lab studies using precisely timed pink noise have shown interesting effects on deep sleep, but playing it all night from a speaker is a different thing, so think of it as a comfortable sleep aid rather than a sleep booster.
