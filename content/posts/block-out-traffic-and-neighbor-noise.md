To block out traffic and neighbor noise, play a steady masking sound matched to the noise you hear: brown noise for low rumbles like trucks, buses and bass through the wall, and pink noise or rain for voices, TV and barking. Masking does not make the outside world silent, but it raises the background level so sudden sounds stand out less. Pair it with a few room fixes, like sealing gaps and adding soft furnishings, for the biggest improvement.

## How sound masking actually works

Your brain is tuned to notice change. A quiet room makes every car door, footstep or raised voice feel like an interruption. A steady background sound reduces the contrast between "quiet" and "noise," so the intrusive sound becomes less noticeable, even though it is still technically there.

The key is **matching**. A masking sound works best when it has energy in the same frequency range as the noise you want to hide. A deep rumble needs a deep masker. A chatty voice needs something in the middle range. That is why one noise color rarely fixes everything, and why mixing layers is so useful.

## Match the noise color to the problem

Here is a practical guide to which sounds tend to cover which disturbances.

- **Traffic, trucks and buses:** mostly low-frequency rumble. **Brown noise** is the best match, with its deep, roaring character.
- **Bass from music or a neighbor's TV:** also very low. Brown noise helps, though very deep bass is difficult to mask fully.
- **Voices, conversations and TV dialogue:** mostly middle frequencies. **Pink noise** or steady **rain** covers this range well.
- **Barking dogs, car horns and sirens:** sharp and sudden, often in the middle and upper range. Pink noise plus rain is a good start, but these are the hardest sounds to hide.
- **Footsteps and thuds from upstairs:** low, impact-style sounds. Brown noise softens them, and so does sleeping further from the shared ceiling or wall.

## Building a city-proof mix

Most city noise is a mixture, so a layered mix often works better than a single noise color. A good starting recipe:

1. **Start with brown noise** as your base layer, set to a moderate level. This handles the rumble.
2. **Add pink noise** at a lower level to fill the middle range where voices live.
3. **Layer rain on top.** Rain adds natural, irregular texture that helps hide the start and end of sudden sounds, and it is more pleasant to listen to for hours than pure noise.
4. **Adjust while listening.** Leave the window open for a minute, or wait for the noise you hate most, and tweak each layer until it fades into the background.

## Where to put the speaker

Speaker placement makes a real difference, and it is often overlooked.

- **Between you and the noise.** If traffic comes through a window, place the speaker on the windowsill or nearby. If voices come through a wall, put it near that wall.
- **Not right next to your head.** A speaker a short distance away creates a more even wash of sound and lets you keep the volume lower.
- **Aim it into the room.** Pointing it toward the source of the noise and the room, rather than directly at your ear, spreads the masking more naturally.

Keep the volume moderate. The goal is to blur the noise, not to cover it with something even louder. As a rough rule, set it just loud enough to blur the noise and no louder.

## Room tweaks that reduce noise

Masking works best when you also reduce how much noise gets in. You do not need a renovation. Small changes can help.

- **Seal gaps.** Sound travels through any opening. Weatherstripping around windows and a draft stopper under the door can make a noticeable difference.
- **Use heavy curtains.** Thick, lined curtains absorb some sound coming through windows and reduce echo in the room.
- **Add soft surfaces.** Rugs, upholstered furniture, bookshelves full of books and soft bedding all absorb sound rather than reflecting it.
- **Move the bed.** If one wall faces the street or a noisy neighbor, sleeping against a different wall can help.
- **Consider earplugs.** For nights when the noise is bad, earplugs plus a low masking sound is a strong combination.

## Neighbors and barking: a few extra thoughts

Neighbor noise is often irregular, which is exactly what makes it stressful. Some practical ideas:

- **Start your masking sound before the noise starts.** If a neighbor's TV usually goes on at a certain time, start your mix beforehand so the change is less noticeable.
- **Use rain for barking.** Barking is sharp and sudden. Rain's irregular patter helps hide the onset of each bark better than a smooth noise alone.
- **Talk to them, kindly.** Many people simply do not realize how much sound carries. A friendly conversation can solve more than any speaker.

If ongoing noise is affecting your sleep, mood or health, it is worth speaking to a doctor, and in some cases your landlord or local council.

## The short version

Match your masking sound to the problem: brown noise for traffic and low rumbles, pink noise or rain for voices and barking, and a layered mix when you hear all of them. Put the speaker between you and the noise, keep the volume moderate, and help it along by sealing gaps, adding curtains and rugs, and using earplugs on the worst nights.
