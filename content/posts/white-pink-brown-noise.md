White, pink and brown noise are all steady, random sounds that contain a wide range of frequencies. The difference is balance: white noise gives every frequency equal energy and sounds bright and hissy, pink noise turns down the highs and sounds like steady rain, and brown noise turns them down even more and sounds like a deep rumble or distant waterfall. For sleep most people prefer pink or brown, for masking sharp noises white is strongest, and for focus it comes down to personal taste.

## How each noise color actually sounds

The "color" names are borrowed loosely from light. White light contains all colors in equal measure, and white noise contains all audible frequencies in equal measure. The other colors describe how that balance tilts.

- **White noise** sounds like TV static, a hissing radiator, or an untuned radio. Because our ears are especially sensitive to higher frequencies, the even spread comes across as bright and a little sharp.
- **Pink noise** sounds like steady rain on a roof, wind through leaves, or a gentle waterfall heard from nearby. The high end is softened, so it feels fuller and calmer than white noise.
- **Brown noise** sounds like a low roar: the inside of an airplane cabin, heavy surf heard from a distance, or a strong river. Most of the energy sits in the bass, and the hiss almost disappears.

The quickest way to understand the difference is to hear it. Open the sound machine and switch between the three noise channels one at a time at the same volume. Most people can tell them apart within a few seconds.

## The technical difference, briefly

If you like the details, here they are without the math. White noise has equal power at every frequency. Pink noise drops in power as frequency rises, so each octave (for example 100 to 200 Hz, then 200 to 400 Hz) carries the same amount of energy. Brown noise drops off faster still, so the low octaves dominate.

That octave balance is why pink noise is often described as sounding "even" to human ears. We hear pitch roughly in octaves, so a sound with equal energy per octave feels balanced rather than tilted toward the top. ## Which noise color is best for sleep

For most adults, sleep is where the differences matter most, because you will be listening for hours.

### Pick white noise if

- You need to cover sharp, high-pitched sounds such as a barking dog, voices through a wall, or a squeaky floor.
- You live somewhere with lots of unpredictable noise and want the strongest masking for the lowest volume.
- You already sleep with a fan and like that kind of sound.

### Pick pink noise if

- White noise feels harsh or tiring after a while.
- You want something that blends into the background and is easy to forget about.
- You like the feeling of falling asleep to rain.

### Pick brown noise if

- Low, rumbling sounds bother you, such as traffic, a neighbor's bass, or a partner's snoring.
- You find any hiss distracting.
- You like the cocooned feeling of a plane cabin or a car on the highway.

## Which is best for focus and studying

Research on background noise and concentration is mixed and depends a lot on the person and the task. Some people find any steady sound helps them settle in, and others find silence works best. Treat it as an experiment rather than a rule.

As a starting point, brown noise is popular for long stretches of reading or writing because it fades into the background. Pink noise is a good middle ground. White noise can feel energizing for short bursts but tiring over a full afternoon. ## Which is best for babies

Steady noise is widely used to help babies settle, and white noise is the most common choice simply because it is what most baby machines play. Pink or brown noise works too, and some parents find them more pleasant to have running in the house.

The color matters much less than how you use it. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises keeping sound machines well away from the crib and at a low volume. A 2014 study in the journal Pediatrics tested a number of infant sleep machines and found that some could exceed recommended noise limits for infants when played at maximum volume and placed close to the crib. Our guide to [white noise for babies](/blog/white-noise-for-babies/) covers safe volume, distance, and how to wean off it. If you have specific concerns about your child's sleep or hearing, talk to your pediatrician.

## Tips for choosing your color

A few habits make it easier to find what works:

1. **Match the volume before comparing.** A louder sound almost always seems "better" at masking, so set each color to a similar, comfortable level before deciding.
2. **Give it a few nights.** The first night with any new sound can feel strange. Your brain usually stops paying attention to it after a short adjustment.
3. **Keep it low.** Use the quietest level that covers the sounds you are trying to block. You want it to blend in, not fill the room.
4. **Try nature sounds too.** Rain, ocean, and streams are naturally pink or brown in character, and many people find them more relaxing than pure noise.
5. **Use a timer if you only need help falling asleep.** Some people prefer sound all night, others only while drifting off.

> If you are unsure where to start, try pink noise. It sits between the other two and suits the widest range of people.

## The short version

White noise is bright and best for masking sharp sounds. Pink noise is softer, more balanced, and a good all-rounder for sleep and relaxation. Brown noise is deep and rumbly, great for covering low sounds and for people who dislike hiss. None of them is medically "better" than the others; the right one is the one you stop noticing.
